Reflection

My evidence comes from the ticket tabs, where I compared what I expected to what actually happened, and from the Cash tab.

1 · How would I evaluate the performance of the agents on each ticket, and why?

Overall, I was impressed with how carefully the team worked. Every number they used came from the shop's real data, no money moved until I approved it, and nothing was sent to a customer or vendor without me. The places where they fell short were less about getting facts wrong and more about judgment and efficiency.

  • Ticket 101 (Bulldog tee): strong. The team figured out that the size S tee was out of stock, found the right vendor to restock it, and noticed something I hadn't expected: that vendor wouldn't ship anything until we paid an overdue $840 bill. It set up that payment and the restock order for me to approve, and drafted a polite update to Tauhid that didn't over-promise a delivery date. My one critique is that the Boss went back to Accounting twice when once should have been enough, so it took more time than a single tee should. The Ticket 101 tab shows each step, and the Cash tab shows the $840 payment taking checking from $3,400 to $2,560.
  • Ticket 102 (Rent due): the best run. This was quick and to the point. Facilities confirmed the $2,400 rent and its due date, Accounting checked we could afford it, and the payment was ready for me to approve. The only hiccups were technical problems on my end (a caching issue and a server restart), not mistakes by the agents. The Ticket 102 tab shows how short the run was, and the Cash tab shows the rent payment taking checking from $2,560 to $160.
  • Ticket 103 (Bulk hoodies): good math, but it overstepped. The team got the numbers right: only 8 of the 20 hoodies were in stock, it ordered the other 12, and the price it offered still made us money. But the Boss decided on a 20% discount by itself and had Customer Service offer it to the club. That's a decision I would have wanted to make myself, after hearing what discount the club was hoping for. It was also the slowest of the three. The Ticket 103 tab shows the whole run, and the Cash tab confirms that none of this moved any cash.

2 · For each ticket, how did the Actual compare to the Expected plan I wrote earlier?

Two of my three plans were close to what the agents actually did. The third, the hoodie discount, went differently from what I had in mind. My Expected and Actual bullets sit side by side on each ticket tab (101, 102, 103), so the differences below are easy to check.

  • Ticket 101: I was right that the Boss would start with Inventory to check the tee was in stock, and that Accounting would be involved. What I didn't predict was that the Boss would also bring in Customer Service, which makes sense in hindsight, since Tauhid needed an update. I also expected the agents to use the broad inventory overview tool, but Inventory used a more specific stock check instead.
  • Ticket 102: This matched my plan almost exactly: Facilities first because it's about rent, then Accounting to handle the payment. The only difference was the tool. Instead of the "payment outgoing" overview I expected, the agents looked up the lease directly and previewed the payment.
  • Ticket 103: This is where my plan and reality split. I expected Inventory to go first; the Boss started with Accounting to work out the discount. I expected Customer Service to ask the club what discount they wanted, and Accounting to check with me, but instead the team picked 20% and offered it. I also said it shouldn't look at sale value yet, and it did.

The bigger lesson is that the agents preferred the more specific tools I added later over the three overview tools I had in mind when I wrote my plans.

3 · What would have been simpler as one agent with tools, and why?

  • The rent ticket (102) really only needed two steps: look up the rent, then pay it. One agent could have done both. Passing it from the Boss to Facilities and then to Accounting added handoffs without adding much, since Facilities mostly repeated what the lease already said (see the Actual section on the Ticket 102 tab).
  • The tee order (101) was also a straight line: check stock, find the vendor, pay the overdue bill, order the restock, message the customer. Splitting it across agents meant information had to be re-explained at each handoff, and Accounting was called twice. It was a lot of back-and-forth for one $28 shirt (the Ticket 101 tab shows Accounting called twice).
  • Why I'd still keep a team: splitting the work has real benefits when money and customers are involved. Only Accounting can make payments, Customer Service never sees our costs, and it's easy to trace who did what. For three small tickets one agent would have been simpler, but as the shop grows, I think the team is the safer setup.

4 · Three new problems Campus Customs might face that this agent team could solve with the tools I built

  • Running out of stock before anyone notices. The Crest Mug is already at zero and some tee sizes are running low. Inventory can spot this on its own, pick the right vendor (mugs come from a different supplier than apparel), and price a restock, and Accounting can write up the order for me to approve. It would also warn me that we only have $160 left, as the Cash tab shows.
  • Next month's rent. My Cash tab ends at $160, but another $2,400 of rent is due October 2. Facilities can flag that it's coming and draft a note to the landlord asking for more time, and Accounting would refuse to pay since there isn't enough cash, which is exactly what should happen.
  • A new bulk order. If another club wanted 50 Yale caps, the team could handle it the same way it handled the hoodies (Ticket 103 tab): check how many we have (40), order the rest, work out a price that still makes money, and draft a reply. This time I'd want them to ask me before naming a discount.

5 · Three new problems Campus Customs might face that this agent team could not solve, and what I'd need

  • Actually getting paid. If the Yale AI Club says yes to the $928 offer, nothing in my system can record the sale, add the money to checking, or take the hoodies off the shelf. As the Cash tab shows, money only ever goes out, and we've already committed $272 in orders with only $160 left. I would need tools to record sales and incoming payments and to update inventory when an order goes out, plus a Sales agent (or a bigger Accounting role) to manage it, with my approval on any discounts or refunds.
  • Receiving deliveries and paying for them. When the tees and hoodies from the two approved purchase orders on the Cash tab arrive on September 5, nothing adds them to our stock or turns the orders into bills we owe. The shop's calendar also never moves forward. I would need tools to receive a shipment, create a bill from an order, and move the date forward, plus a Receiving agent to check that what arrived matches what we ordered.
  • Actually talking to customers. The messages to Tauhid and the Yale AI Club are still sitting as drafts (see the Actual sections on the Ticket 101 and Ticket 103 tabs), and the agents can't read the club's reply about the discount. New requests can't become tickets on their own either. I would need tools to read incoming email, send a message only after I approve it, and create new tickets, plus a Communications agent to connect replies back to the right ticket.
